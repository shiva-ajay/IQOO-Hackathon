# FixLens: Round 2 pitch — opener + tough questions

This is a follow-on to [PRESENTATION_SCRIPT.md](PRESENTATION_SCRIPT.md), built from the jury's
Round 1 feedback: open stronger in the first 15 seconds, and have simple, confident answers ready
for "how often would you use this?" and "what about Gemini?".

Everything below is written in plain, spoken words on purpose. Practice it out loud, not silently.

---

## 1. The first 15 seconds (the hook)

Say this before you show any slide, or right as slide 1 appears. Then move into the Round 1 script.

**Say (recommended, ~15 sec):**
> Your washing machine flashes "E4" at midnight. No manual, no technician, no internet.
> You point your phone at it, ask out loud — and FixLens shows you the exact part to touch,
> right there, in seconds. That's FixLens: point, ask, fix.

**Tip:** Say it a little slower than feels natural. Pause after "no internet." and after "in
seconds." Those two pauses are what make it land. Practice with a timer — it should take
14 to 17 seconds, not less.

### Two alternates, if you want to try a different angle

**Alternate A — leads with the car (more visual, good if judges saw the demo photos):**
> A car that won't start. A washing machine blinking a code nobody understands. Right now,
> your only options are: call someone, wait, and pay. FixLens lets you point your phone,
> ask out loud, and it shows you exactly what to touch — no internet, no manual, in seconds.

**Alternate B — leads with a question, forces engagement:**
> Quick question: how many things in your house or car have you ignored because you didn't
> know where to even start? FixLens fixes that. Point your phone at it, ask out loud, and
> it shows you the exact part to touch — completely offline.

**Tip:** Pick one and stick to it. Don't say all three. If you're not sure which lands better,
say Alternate A to a friend and the recommended one to another friend, and see which one makes
them say "oh, that's useful" first.

## 2. "How often would you actually use this in real life?"

This question means: *is this a toy, or does it actually matter day to day?* Don't get
defensive. Answer with a comparison first, then widen it.

**Soundbite (say this first, memorize it word for word):**
> You don't use a fire extinguisher every day. But the day something catches fire, you're
> very glad it's right there. FixLens is that, for anything in your home or car that
> suddenly stops working.

**Then, if they want more (say in your own words):**
> But it's actually more often than that. Think about everything in one house: the car, the
> washing machine, the fridge, the geyser, the water purifier, the AC. Across all of that,
> something acts up almost every month for most families. And it's not only for
> emergencies — the car-check entries, like checking oil or coolant, are things people
> should do anyway, every few weeks.
>
> And for someone like a building's maintenance staff, who looks after many flats, this
> isn't occasional at all — it's a daily tool. One person, many appliances, many error
> codes, no manual for any of them.

**One-line closer, if they push further:**
> So the value isn't "I open this app every day." It's "the one time I actually need it, I'm
> not stuck." That's exactly when a technician, a queue, or a lost manual would have cost me
> the most time and money.

## 3. "What if Gemini brings an offline model like this — what's the difference then?"

This is the big one. Don't panic and don't put Gemini down — sound confident, not scared.
The core idea: **the AI model is just the engine. FixLens is the whole car.**

**Soundbite (memorize this one):**
> Gemini, or any AI model, is just the brain. It's smart, but a smart brain can still guess
> wrong, especially about something like wiring or torque values — and a wrong guess during
> a repair is dangerous. FixLens is built so the AI never guesses. It only speaks steps that
> I've personally checked and put in a verified knowledge base. On top of that, FixLens
> tracks the exact part in real time as you move the phone — that's a separate engineering
> problem a general chatbot doesn't solve.

**Then, if they push (say in your own words):**
> Think of it like GPS. Everyone has access to GPS satellites, but Google Maps is valuable
> because of the routing, the traffic data, the app built on top. It's the same here. If
> Gemini becomes available offline tomorrow, that's actually good news for me — I can plug
> it in as a better brain. My system is already built in two parts: the "brain" that
> understands images, and everything around it — the checked repair steps, the safety
> rules, and the live part-tracking. That second part is the actual product, and it doesn't
> go away just because someone else has a good model too.

**One-line closer, if they push further:**
> So it's not FixLens versus Gemini. Gemini is a tool I could use inside FixLens. The
> product is the trust and the tracking built around it — that's what doesn't come for free
> with any model, however good it is.

## 4. How to handle a tough question you didn't expect

Simple 3-step habit, works for almost anything they throw at you:

1. **Repeat it back in your own words, in one short sentence.** This buys you a second to think,
   and shows you understood. ("So you're asking whether this still matters once bigger models
   go offline — got it.")
2. **Give the one-sentence answer first.** Not the story, the conclusion. Jury members remember
   the first sentence of your answer far more than the fifth.
3. **Then explain, in two or three more sentences, if they still look unsure.** Stop once they nod.

If you genuinely don't know the answer: say "That's a fair point, I haven't tested that yet" —
plainly, no over-explaining. Judges trust an honest "not yet" far more than a stretched answer.
